# Clarity Creates Capacity: Research Comparison

## Purpose

Second pass of external-research checking against a Northlight core principle, following the same treatment run on "Organisations Are Living Systems" (`EVD-EXT-0001`). This one surfaces real friction, not just alignment — worth preserving as a contradiction per the repository's own evidence philosophy ("Preserve Contradictions": contradictory evidence reveals hidden assumptions and should be explored, not discarded).

## The relevant research

**Cynefin's "Clear domain" trap.** Dave Snowden's Cynefin framework treats "Clear" (sometimes called "Obvious" or "Simple") as one of five decision domains, where cause and effect are well understood and best practice applies. The framework's most-cited warning is about the boundary between Clear and Chaotic: organizations that settle into the Clear domain — confident, standardized, sure of their own best practices — become complacent and are prone to catastrophic collapse when the environment shifts underneath them, because the system's success in the Clear domain suppresses the sensing needed to notice the shift. Snowden's broader caution: complex problems, where cause and effect are only knowable in retrospect, get misclassified as complicated or clear because clarity is more comfortable to act on than ambiguity. The prescribed approach for genuinely complex situations is probe–sense–respond — small, safe-to-fail experiments — not the direct clarify-then-execute sequence that works for complicated problems.

**Karl Weick's sensemaking.** In *Sensemaking in Organizations* (1995) and earlier work, Weick argues that people in organizations often cannot think their way to clarity before acting — "How can I know what I think until I see what I say?" Meaning is frequently constructed retrospectively, through action and talk, rather than established in advance and then executed against. Sensemaking prioritizes plausibility and coherence over accuracy; demanding clarity too early, before enough cues exist to interpret, can produce a confident but wrong account of the situation.

**Ashby's law of requisite variety.** From cybernetics: a system's ability to regulate or respond to its environment is bounded by its own internal variety — "only variety can absorb variety." Organizations have historically coped with environmental complexity by simplifying it, but oversimplification causes unfamiliar signals to be misclassified as familiar ones. When an operating environment is highly variable, reducing internal complexity below what the environment demands doesn't create capacity — it removes the repertoire of responses needed to actually handle what shows up.

## Where Northlight's framework already anticipates this

`frameworks/organisational-clarity-framework.md` and the worldview document already hedge against the crudest version of this critique: "Clarity is not simplification for its own sake. It is removing unnecessary complexity so that people can make better decisions." That's the right instinct — the word "unnecessary" is doing real work. The open question the research raises is operational, not philosophical: how does a practitioner tell "unnecessary" complexity (safe to remove) from "requisite" variety (the organization's actual capacity to handle an unpredictable environment) before removing it?

## Where the friction is real

The clarity framework's five-step sequence — clarify outcomes, ownership, decisions, information flow, measures — is a Clear/Complicated-domain move: it assumes the problem in front of the client is knowable through analysis, has a right answer once analyzed, and can be acted on in that order. That's often true, and the framework's track record (faster decisions, less rework, better alignment) reflects real complicated-domain wins. But applied to a genuinely complex situation — new market, ambiguous mandate, a team still discovering what the actual problem is — clarifying ownership and measures too early can manufacture false confidence, foreclose the sensing Cynefin says the situation needs, and strip out the variety (redundant roles, informal workarounds, unresolved ambiguity) the organization may actually need to metabolize what's coming.

## Practical implication

The clarity framework needs a domain check before step one, not after: is this problem complicated (clarity work is the right move) or complex (probe-sense-respond first, clarify what emerges)? Northlight's existing instinct — "observe before you intervene," "multiple perspectives can be true" — already does the sensing Weick and Snowden are describing. The gap isn't in the diagnostic posture; it's that the clarity framework, once triggered, doesn't currently have a step that asks whether clarity is the right medicine yet.
