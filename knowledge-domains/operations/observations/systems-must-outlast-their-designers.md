# Systems must outlast their designers to be worth building

## Observation

"Will this still make sense after I'm gone?" is a useful design question for any operational system. Systems built to make sense only to the people who designed them — held together by tacit knowledge, individual memory, or the designer's continued presence — are not systems. They are personally managed complexity.

The systems most worth building are understandable, documented, and usable by people who did not create them. They reduce cognitive load rather than increasing it. Good leadership is measurable, in part, by what remains legible after the leader is no longer in the room.

## Interpretation

This has direct implications for how operational handoffs are designed, how documentation decisions are made, and how knowledge is treated as an organisational asset rather than personal property. A system that requires its designer present to function creates a bottleneck that will reappear whenever the designer is unavailable.

The question is not whether the system is elegant — it is whether someone else can run it. Co-developing systems with the people who will use them is often the fastest path to systems that outlast their origin.

## Evidence Needed

- Examples where systems designed without end-users failed at transition points (role changes, departures, scale).
- Evidence on the relationship between co-design involvement and system longevity.
- Counterexamples: systems designed by individuals that survived transition well because of documentation quality alone.
