# Source Material & Reading Notes

## Book

*Cameron Herold, The Second in Command: Unleash the Power of Your COO* (2023).

The publisher/author positioning makes the intended audience clear in retrospect: the book is primarily a guide for CEOs/founders who are deciding whether to hire a second-in-command, defining the role, hiring, onboarding, and working with that person. Elizabeth initially experienced the audience as ambiguous because much of the early book describes the COO's identity, characteristics, and work in language that strongly resonated with an experienced second-in-command.

---

# The initial reaction

The book produced an unusually strong feeling of recognition.

Elizabeth's immediate reaction was essentially:

> "I feel seen as a COO."

The book put language around experiences that Elizabeth had already lived:
- the fluidity of the role;
- the fact that "COO" is often a function rather than a title;
- the importance of complementarity;
- the fact that the same person may be an excellent COO in one organization and the wrong COO in another;
- the need to adapt the role to the CEO and the organization's stage;
- execution as a form of executive leadership;
- systems thinking;
- curiosity and generalist capability;
- relational trust;
- building conditions for other people to succeed;
- working oneself out of a role as the organization grows.

The validation mattered because this is a form of leadership that can be highly consequential while remaining relatively invisible.

Elizabeth is a voracious reader and will read almost anything: business, history, fantasy, classics, religion, biography, and more. She jokes that she will even read a shampoo bottle or cereal box, in English or French, if it gives her something to read.

# The surprise of the physical book

Elizabeth originally believed she had signed up for an e-book. She was frustrated when no download arrived.

Then Amazon delivered a physical book to her door.

She did not initially know what the package was. It turned out to be the book.

She sat down and started reading almost immediately and kept the book nearby.

This is potentially useful color for a future review because it explains the intensity of the response: the book was not assigned reading. It became an unexpected discovery.

---

# Initial learning-log reaction sent to Cameron Herold's team

Elizabeth wrote that the book described:
- the fluidity of the COO role;
- the different hats a COO wears depending on the day;
- how being a COO in one organization does not automatically qualify someone to be COO in another;
- how someone can be excellent at one organizational size but not another;
- how a person's gifts may take an organization to one stage and another person may need to take it further;
- why that is okay.

She described herself as having been a COO "albeit with different titles" in small organizations and as technically the CEO of Northlight Advisory Services while recognizing that, as a one-person company, she occupies every role.

Cameron's team responded that her observation about organizational size particularly resonated and offered to connect her with the COO Alliance's VP of Operations.

---

# Audience ambiguity

At first Elizabeth wondered whether the book was:
1. for CEOs hiring COOs;
2. for COOs learning the role;
3. for people considering the COO Alliance;
4. or all three.

By the end of the book, she concluded it is fundamentally a CEO-oriented book.

That is not a flaw in itself. It is simply important context.

The strongest CEO-facing sections are:
- whether to hire a COO;
- defining the role;
- choosing a title;
- hiring;
- onboarding;
- delegation;
- working relationship;
- conflict;
- recognizing when the COO no longer fits the organization.

The COO reader nevertheless receives substantial value because the book reveals what CEOs are being told to expect from the role.

# Part One â€” The COO

## The role is difficult to define

Elizabeth strongly agreed with Herold's premise that COO is one of the hardest executive roles to define.

A CFO has a recognizable domain. A CMO has a recognizable domain. A COO may be responsible for almost anything the CEO needs them to own.

Therefore:

**The COO role is defined relationally and contextually.**

The title may be:
- COO;
- President;
- VP Operations;
- Director of Operations;
- Operations Manager;
- General Manager;
- Chief of Staff;
- or another title.

The title matters for compensation and external expectations, but the underlying function can remain "second-in-command."

---

# The second-in-command as complement

The strongest insight for Elizabeth was complementarity.

The CEO should not hire a mirror image.

The second-in-command should:
- cover weaknesses;
- absorb work that drains the CEO;
- challenge ideas privately;
- turn vision into execution;
- integrate functions;
- remove obstacles;
- build systems;
- grow people;
- create organizational capacity.

Elizabeth's own shorthand:

**CEO: moves things forward.  
COO: makes it possible for things to move forward.**

---

# The seven types of COO

Elizabeth found the categories useful, especially:
- Executor;
- Change Agent;
- Mentor;
- Other Half;
- Partner;
- Heir Apparent;
- MVP.

She did not identify equally with every type.

She particularly recognized herself in:
- Executor;
- Other Half;
- Integrator;
- Moderator;
- Enforcer.

She is less naturally a visionary. Once she believes in a vision, however, she is very effective at making it real.

---

# Generalist rather than expert

Elizabeth strongly agreed that a COO does not need to be the deepest expert in every domain.

The COO needs:
- intellectual curiosity;
- enough domain knowledge to ask good questions;
- the ability to understand how functions connect;
- the ability to find the right expert;
- the ability to create the conditions in which experts can succeed.

She describes herself as a strong generalist.

She can have intelligent conversations about:
- technology;
- AI;
- systems;
- Salesforce;
- Python;
- operations;
- finance;
- training;
- governance;
- marketing;
- organizational design;

without needing to be the technical expert in each.

The COO's job is not to know everything.

**The COO needs to know how to find out what matters and who can solve it.**

---

# Question-asking as executive capability

Elizabeth sees questioning as a core skill.

The COO can ask:
- What are we actually trying to accomplish?
- Why are we doing this?
- What would have to be true for this to work?
- What are we missing?
- What is the consequence of this decision?
- What happens next?
- What system allowed this problem to happen?
- Is this actually the right idea, or merely a good idea?

The COO does not have to supply every answer.

Sometimes the best leadership is helping another person arrive at the answer.

---

# Strategic slowing-down

Elizabeth particularly liked the idea that a COO sometimes has to slow the CEO down.

This is not obstruction.

It is discernment.

There may be hundreds of good ideas. The organization needs to identify the **right** idea at the **right** time.

Elizabeth uses the concept of a parking lot for ideas:
- capture the idea;
- preserve it;
- stop it from distracting current work;
- let it mature;
- revisit it later.

She sees this as different from simply rejecting ideas.

---

# Energy and organizational climate

Herold discusses the COO's energy and its impact on the organization.

Elizabeth agreed strongly.

She has observed that:
- the first message of the day can affect her;
- the first meeting can affect the tone of a day;
- the emotional state of leaders affects teams;
- team energy can also affect leaders.

She related the book's barista analogy to working from home:

**Her barista is herself.**

The same principle applies to CEOs, COOs, and leadership teams.

---

# Systems that survive the operator

This became one of Elizabeth's strongest examples.

At Divine Renovation, Elizabeth historically took December completely offline.

Her normal vacation was not always a true disconnect. She might check messages or remain reachable.

December was different:
- email came off her phone;
- computer was shut down;
- she did not reopen it until the new year;
- only a tiny number of people had a way to reach her.

Therefore every system she built had to work without her.

She did not merely claim to build scalable systems.

She stress-tested them by disappearing.

This is a particularly strong example for future writing:

**A system is not truly operationally mature if it requires its creator to remain available.**

---

# Building systems that outlast you

Herold argues that a COO helps position the company for the CEO's eventual exit.

Elizabeth expands the principle:

The same logic applies to the COO.

She intentionally built event systems at Divine Renovation that could continue without her.

She reviewed them with the team, gathered feedback, debated what worked and what did not, and refined them collaboratively.

After leaving, she learned that teams were still using systems she had built.

This is evidence of operational success.

---

# Trust and the CEO/COO relationship

Elizabeth strongly agreed that the CEO and COO relationship requires unusual trust.

The COO may be:
- the CEO's confidant;
- the person who hears fears;
- the person who hears doubts;
- the person who can challenge the CEO;
- the person who makes the CEO's vision executable;
- the person who can tell the CEO uncomfortable truths.

The relationship can become deeply personal.

That creates both strength and risk.

Elizabeth believes the relationship can become intimate enough that boundaries matter.

The spouses/partners of the CEO and COO also matter.

A healthy model requires:
- trust;
- respect;
- personal connection;
- relational depth;
- psychological safety;
- clear boundaries.

She strongly rejects the book's "date night" terminology for CEO/COO relationships, particularly across genders, even though she agrees that intentional relationship-building matters.

---

# The CEO does not owe the COO loyalty

This is an important distinction Elizabeth developed during reflection.

The COO is the CEO's person.

The COO is the person the CEO calls at midnight saying:

**"Bring a shovel."**

But the CEO is not necessarily the COO's equivalent trusted confidant.

The relationship is asymmetrical.

The CEO can be vulnerable to the COO.

The COO may not have an equivalent person above them.

This makes the role psychologically demanding.

---

# What does the CEO owe the COO?

Elizabeth's answer evolved into:

- honesty;
- integrity;
- fair pay;
- inclusion;
- communication;
- access to the information needed to do the job;
- respect for the COO's authority;
- staying out of the COO's work once it has been delegated;
- honest conversation when the relationship or role changes.

The CEO does not owe the COO permanent employment or loyalty.

But if the CEO has chosen to create this relationship, they should treat it as a privilege and responsibility rather than as disposable labor.

---

# What does the COO owe the CEO?

The COO owes:
- loyalty to the agreed vision;
- honest challenge;
- execution;
- discretion;
- confidentiality;
- the willingness to make the CEO look good;
- the willingness to let the CEO receive the public credit;
- the ability to accept that the CEO makes the final call.

The COO is not the CEO.

Sometimes the CEO makes a decision the COO disagrees with.

The COO's job is generally to implement it.

There are exceptions for decisions that are:
- illegal;
- materially unethical;
- catastrophically damaging;
- or so far outside the organization's purpose that intervention becomes necessary.

But those are exceptional cases.

---

# Ego vs sense of self

Elizabeth disagrees somewhat with language about the COO needing a "strong ego."

She prefers:

**The COO needs a strong sense of self, but a willingness to surrender ego.**

The COO must be confident enough to:
- carry responsibility;
- challenge the CEO;
- make difficult decisions;
- stand behind work.

But the COO must also be willing to:
- remain in the background;
- let the CEO receive the spotlight;
- measure success by organizational outcomes;
- avoid public recognition as a primary motivator.

Elizabeth does not particularly enjoy public praise.

She gets satisfaction from knowing:
- the organization succeeded;
- the system worked;
- people grew;
- the vision became reality;
- the success was possible because the right conditions were created.

---

# Servant leadership

Elizabeth strongly identified with Herold's upside-down org-chart concept.

The CEO and COO are not necessarily "at the top."

They are support structures.

Their role is to create the conditions in which others can succeed.

Elizabeth sees this as consistent with servant leadership.

---

# Training as culture-building

One of Elizabeth's strongest personal examples was a two-day ClickUp training session at Divine Renovation.

The training was intentionally designed as more than software instruction.

It included:
- a team brunch;
- relationship-building;
- slides and technical instruction;
- relevant examples;
- project scoping;
- small-group work;
- post-it-note exercises;
- recurring finance workflows;
- board-package processes;
- conversations with founders;
- conversations with visiting priests;
- shared meals;
- Mass;
- cultural storytelling;
- discussion about why the organization existed and why it operated the way it did.

The goal was not merely "teach ClickUp."

The goal was:

**Teach a tool, redesign work, build culture, build relationships, and help people see how the tool could make their lives better.**

Elizabeth watched an accounting team member move from passive participation to genuine excitement because she could see how the system would improve her work.

This is a signature example of Elizabeth's approach to operations.

---

# Fractional COO

Elizabeth found Herold's discussion of fractional COOs particularly relevant.

A fractional COO can:
- coach an executive team;
- lead a core project;
- solve a defined operational problem;
- provide execution capacity;
- create clarity;
- test whether a company actually needs a full-time COO;
- help a CEO understand what they need before making a permanent hire.

This validates Elizabeth's current fractional COO direction.

---

# Organizational stages and working yourself out of a job

One of the most significant shifts in Elizabeth's thinking was Herold's argument that a COO may only be the right person for a particular stage of the company's growth.

A company may double once with one COO, again with the same COO, and then need a different operating leader for the next stage.

Elizabeth recognized this in her own career.

At Divine Renovation:
- she helped build and scale the organization;
- the organization grew;
- eventually the complexity and needs changed;
- another executive role was introduced that overlapped with some of her responsibilities;
- she remained but functionally operated as a "shadow COO" in some respects.

Her retrospective conclusion:

**The CFO appointment itself was not necessarily the moment she should have left.**

The moment she should have left was when she asked to clarify the overlap between her role and the incoming CFO's role and repeatedly could not get a meaningful conversation.

That was the signal of relational/role failure.

---

# Signs the CEO/COO relationship is ending

Elizabeth identified several signals.

From the CEO side:
- the CEO stops including the COO in important conversations;
- the CEO begins sharing key information with someone else;
- another person starts occupying the CEO's office/attention;
- the CEO permits repeated boundary violations;
- the COO's authority becomes undermined;
- the CEO begins second-guessing or bypassing the COO.

From the COO side:
- confidence in the CEO declines;
- frustration becomes chronic;
- backchanneling becomes negative rather than constructive;
- the COO starts looking for evidence that the CEO is wrong;
- the COO stops having fun;
- work becomes a slog;
- routines that maintained connection disappear;
- there is fundamental disagreement with the direction of the organization.

A critical observation:

**The end often begins before either person formally acknowledges it.**

---

# Mistakes and trust

Elizabeth liked the "deposits before withdrawals" metaphor.

Trust creates a reservoir.

When mistakes occur:
- trust makes forgiveness possible;
- context makes mistakes understandable;
- prior investment in people gives leaders room to recover.

Without trust, every mistake becomes evidence.

---

# Onboarding

Elizabeth strongly agreed with the 90-day approach:
- Month 1: observe, listen, attend, take notes;
- Month 2: identify possible changes and stress-test assumptions;
- Month 3: begin making changes based on accumulated understanding.

A COO should not arrive and immediately "fix everything."

They need:
- relationships;
- history;
- context;
- trust;
- knowledge of why systems exist;
- awareness of informal organizational dynamics.

Some of the most valuable information may come from unexpected people.

---

# Culture and systems are connected

Elizabeth rejects the idea that onboarding is simply procedural.

Good onboarding includes:
- mandatory baseline requirements;
- role-specific training;
- cultural language;
- organizational history;
- relationships;
- context;
- role-specific customization.

She drew from retail experience to illustrate the difference between:
1. universal baseline training;
2. role-specific training;
3. management development;
4. individualized development.

---

# The CEO's active inventory

Elizabeth liked the "active inventory" exercise.

Tasks are classified roughly as:
- incompetent;
- competent;
- excellent;
- unique ability.

She believes the concept is useful for CEOs and COOs alike.

A COO should also ask:
- What am I excellent at?
- What drains me?
- What could someone else do?
- What should I stop doing?
- What creates energy?
- What creates organizational value?

The objective is not simply to do more.

It is to allocate human capability intelligently.

---

# The CEO's job after hiring the COO

Elizabeth's strongest summary of the CEO-facing portion:

**Delegate everything except genius.**

The CEO should use the freed capacity to:
- think;
- envision;
- connect externally;
- develop strategy;
- build relationships;
- pursue the things only they can do.

Hiring a COO is a privilege because someone else is taking responsibility away from the CEO.

The CEO should not resent that.

---

# The COO's job after hiring

The COO should:
- build the team;
- grow people;
- delegate;
- build systems;
- develop organizational capacity;
- protect the CEO's focus;
- execute the vision;
- identify problems;
- create conditions for others to succeed.

The COO's work is never completely finished.

The objective is not to become indispensable.

The objective is to make the organization increasingly capable without you.

---

# Working yourself out of the role

This became one of Elizabeth's strongest conclusions.

**A good COO should work themselves out of the role.**

If the company grows successfully:
- the company's needs change;
- the COO's strengths may stop matching the new needs;
- the organization may need a different operating profile;
- the COO may need to move on.

That is not necessarily failure.

It can be evidence that the COO succeeded.

This reframes career progression for Elizabeth.

Rather than viewing a COO role as something to hold indefinitely, she can see it as a stage-based executive contribution.

---

# Why fractional COO work is strategically attractive

If a COO works themselves out of roles, then relying on one employer creates concentration risk.

Fractional COO work can provide:
- multiple CEO relationships;
- multiple organizations;
- multiple growth stages;
- variety;
- a way to contribute without waiting for one company to create the next challenge;
- resilience when one engagement ends.

This is especially relevant to Elizabeth's Northlight model.

She recognizes that fractional work has a deadline-driven economic reality:
- income must cover periods between engagements;
- savings/runway matter;
- finding the right next CEO takes time.

Therefore compensation is not merely about today's salary. It must also fund future flexibility.

---

# The book's limitations

Elizabeth's final assessment is broadly positive.

She does **not** think Herold gets much fundamentally wrong.

Her criticisms are mostly about:
- occasional overextension of metaphors;
- the somewhat awkward "date night" framing;
- "strong ego" language where "strong sense of self" is more accurate;
- occasional scattershot audience targeting;
- repeated COO Alliance promotion;
- some areas where a COO reader might want more depth.

She believes the book is fundamentally a CEO book.

For CEOs, she believes it is highly useful.

For COOs, it is useful partly because it reveals:
- what CEOs need;
- what CEOs are being taught to expect;
- why the relationship works;
- why the relationship fails;
- how the role changes over time.

---

# Final personal conclusion

The book validated that executive operations work is a strong fit for Elizabeth.

It reinforced:
- her generalist orientation;
- her curiosity;
- her ability to move between strategic and tactical levels;
- her ability to build systems;
- her ability to translate vision into execution;
- her comfort with invisible leadership;
- her ability to challenge while remaining loyal;
- her preference for values-aligned organizations;
- her natural fit as a second-in-command.

Most importantly, it gave her a new framework for something she had previously experienced differently:

**Working yourself out of a job is not a failure of the COO. It may be the measure of a successful COO.**

That realization changes how she can think about future roles, including fractional COO work.
