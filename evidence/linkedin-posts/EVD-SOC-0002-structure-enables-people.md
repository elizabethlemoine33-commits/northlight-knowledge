# Structure Enables People — LinkedIn Posts

## Summary

A cluster of posts developing the argument that operational structure is not a constraint on people — it is what enables them. The posts trace this from personal philosophy to client patterns: organisations that move from heroic problem-solving to repeatable practice, systems that must outlast the people who designed them, and the structural explanation for why hiring someone competent doesn't automatically clear the bottleneck.

## Source

- Channel: Elizabeth Anne (Randell) Lemoine, LinkedIn personal profile
- Retrieved via: Buffer API
- Date range: February 2026 – July 2026
- Post count: ~9 substantive posts in this cluster

## Key Claims

- Growing organisations must move from heroic problem-solving to repeatable practice — not because people care less, but because the work deserves to last; structures that don't depend on burnout to succeed
- "Will this still make sense after I'm gone?" — the systems to be proudest of are understandable, documented, and usable by others; they reduce cognitive load rather than increasing it; good leadership is measured by what remains legible for the people who come next
- Systems fail not because they're poorly designed on paper but because people can't see how they fit into them; the most effective systems were often co-developed with the people who had to use them
- When a project tone shifts — conversations get careful, emails get longer — it almost never means the people are the problem; it usually means the structure is (roles not defined clearly, accountability landed somewhere nobody agreed to)
- Hiring someone competent is supposed to solve the problem; it doesn't — not automatically; the bottleneck doesn't move, it multiplies; structural work that most founders skip because no one told them it was part of the job
- "The only person in the room who can make the call" — not because no one else is capable but because no one else has the context; all of that context lives in one head, and somehow that became the job
- Clear expectations let people take ownership and often produce work better than you would have done yourself — not because you stepped in, but because they had what they needed
- First question in every engagement: "If this goes well, what will be different in six months?" — not deliverables, not metrics, but what is materially different for the people doing the work and how decisions get made; this becomes the reference point for everything else
