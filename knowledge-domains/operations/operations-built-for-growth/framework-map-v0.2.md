# Operations Built for Growth — Conceptual Framework Map v0.3

**Status: Draft. Research pass completed 2026-08-09. Not ratified Northlight doctrine.**

## 1. Central thesis

> **Operations is the backbone of an organization: the infrastructure and nervous system through which people, information, decisions, commitments, and learning move.**

**Operations Built for Growth** means designing that backbone not merely to support the organization that exists today, but to help the organization become what it is capable of becoming — while retaining the ability to respond intelligently when reality changes.

The objective is not maximum rigidity, maximum efficiency, or theoretical 10× scalability. The objective is an organization with sufficient structure to protect what matters and sufficient **adaptive capacity** to sense change, respond appropriately, learn, and continue becoming.

---

## 2. The central metaphor: the organization as an interconnected living system

The organization is not a collection of independent departments. It is an interconnected system made of specialized functions whose work, information, decisions, and outcomes affect one another.

Operations may be a department, but operational thinking cannot be confined to that department.

A useful working metaphor is:

- **Strategy / leadership** — the brain: direction, intent, priorities, decisions.
- **Operations** — the spine / nervous system: pathways through which information, decisions, commitments, and responses move.
- **Marketing** — blood / flow: carries external intelligence, attention, demand, and signals through the system.
- **Finance** — the system that measures and manages financial reality and resource flow.
- **Sales** — converts market interest into commitments and revenue.
- **Delivery / product / service** — turns commitments into outcomes.
- **People / culture** — the human system through which all of the above actually operates.

The metaphor is a systems model, not a literal organizational taxonomy.

---

## 3. The ascending spiral

The framework is intentionally a **spiral**, not a circle.

A circle implies returning to the same place. The intended model is that every iteration begins from a different position because the organization has learned something.

### **AWARENESS → SENSE → BUILD → CONNECT → LEARN → ↑**

The spiral repeats, but it does not reset.

### Awareness — notice what is happening

Look beyond the immediate function:

1. beyond the individual's role;
2. across the organization;
3. toward clients and the market;
4. toward the wider world.

Awareness asks:

> **What is happening around us?**

Awareness is deliberately broader than marketing awareness. It includes changes in technology, regulation, customer behaviour, financial reality, organizational capacity, competitive conditions, and the lived experience of the people doing the work.

### Sense — understand what it means

Use curiosity, questions, observation, data, conversations, and subject-matter expertise to understand what is actually happening.

Sense asks:

- What does this mean?
- What are we missing?
- Which assumption may no longer be true?
- Where is the actual constraint?
- Is this a symptom or a cause?
- What do the people closest to the work know?

The COO's role is often not to know the answer but to ask questions that help the organization find it.

### Build — turn understanding into capability

Build the process, system, policy, technology, training, data structure, governance, dashboard, role, or operating rhythm that makes the organization more capable.

Build asks:

> **What needs to change, and what will help the organization become what it is becoming?**

Build for what comes next, without over-engineering an imaginary future.

### Connect — make the change work across the system

A solution that works in one department but creates a problem somewhere else is not a successful operational solution.

Connect asks:

- Who needs to know?
- Who owns this?
- What other system is affected?
- Does the information reach the right place?
- Does the promise have a path to delivery?
- Does the fix create another silo?
- Are the right people involved?

Connectivity is not simply a stage. It is a test applied throughout the spiral.

### Learn — turn experience into organizational memory

Learn asks:

- What happened?
- Why?
- What did we get right?
- What did we get wrong?
- What changed?
- What should we do differently next time?
- What did the client / customer / team experience?

A failure should produce learning, not merely an apology or a workaround.

The preferred response is:

**own → acknowledge → diagnose → change → communicate → implement → verify → learn**

The next turn of the spiral begins with what the organization learned.

## 4. Five principles

### 1. See the Whole System

The work is interconnected even when the org chart is not. Understand the relationships between strategy, finance, marketing, sales, delivery, technology, people, data, and clients.

### 2. Build for What the Organization Is Becoming

Do not build only for today's volume, team, tools, or customer base. Build enough capacity and capability to move toward the strategic future while preserving adaptive capacity when reality differs from the plan.

### 3. Connect the Organization to Reality

Bring internal operating reality together with financial reality, client reality, market intelligence, technological change, regulation, and the wider environment.

### 4. Create Clarity, Trust, and Accountability

People need to know what is expected, where to go for answers, what they can change, what they cannot change, and that problems will be addressed honestly. Trust is strengthened by appropriate verification, not by either micromanagement or blind faith.

### 5. Turn Experience into a Spiral of Learning

Successes, failures, client feedback, operational friction, and unexpected events should make the organization more capable than it was before.

## 5. Conditions surrounding the spiral

These are not steps. They are conditions that make the spiral work. Curiosity and humility belong together: curiosity asks the question; humility admits that we do not already know the answer.

- curiosity + intellectual humility (research label: *learning orientation*; Dweck);
- trust;
- clarity;
- psychological safety **and** accountability — both are required co-conditions. High psychological safety without accountability produces a comfort zone, not a learning zone. High accountability without psychological safety produces an anxiety zone where problems get hidden. The learning zone (Edmondson, 1999, 2018) requires both simultaneously.
- appropriate risk-taking;
- willingness to innovate;
- willingness to ask for help;
- willingness to say "I don't know";
- willingness to challenge an assumption.

A practitioner observation from the interview is that the operating leader's own posture influences the organization's posture — and that this relationship is bidirectional. Research on emotional contagion (Hatfield, Cacioppo & Rapson, 1993) and group affective tone (George, 1995) supports the directional claim that leaders' emotional states transfer to teams. The bidirectionality — that a leader who genuinely pays attention to and cares for their team will also be affected by the team's state — is retained as an important feature of the model, not a complication. Leadership is a relationship, not a one-way broadcast. This is an observation with research support, not yet established doctrine at the specific COO level.

## 6. Adaptive systems, not merely strong systems

The framework deliberately prefers **adaptive** to **stronger** systems.

Some systems need firm controls because the organization must protect something non-negotiable: privacy, security, financial integrity, legal obligations, charitable status, tax compliance, contracts, or material organizational risk.

Other systems need to evolve as the organization, technology, market, and customer expectations change.

The desired quality is therefore:

> **Enough structure to protect what matters, and enough adaptive capacity to respond intelligently when reality changes.**

Adaptive does not mean loose or improvised. It implies a deliberate capability to sense, evaluate, change, and learn.

## 7. The Market-Aware COO

### Working definition

> **A Market-Aware COO is a generalist integrator who looks beyond the boundaries of their own function — across the organization, toward clients and the market, and outward toward a changing world — then turns what they learn into better decisions, adaptive systems, and greater organizational capability.**

The COO does not need to be the subject-matter expert in finance, marketing, technology, sales, or delivery.

The COO needs enough understanding to:

- ask good questions;
- understand answers;
- connect functions;
- identify seams and dependencies;
- challenge assumptions;
- recognize risk;
- know when to involve the expert;
- and help the organization act on what it learns.

A strong COO also uses an inquiry-based operating style: asking questions from different angles that help the subject-matter expert surface what is missing, rather than rushing to provide the answer. The COO may guide someone toward a conclusion, but the aim is often to help them see and articulate it themselves. Elizabeth recognizes this pattern in the way Paul Donovan taught and coached people. The closest named framework for this practice is Edgar Schein's *humble inquiry* (2013) — asking questions you genuinely do not know the answer to, as distinct from questions designed to steer toward a predetermined conclusion. This is not the historical Socratic method, which was structured around exposing internal contradictions and often ended in deliberate confusion; what is described here is oriented toward helping, not unsettling.

The COO is often a chameleon: executor, integrator, moderator, enforcer, devil's advocate, mentor, counterpart, or second-in-command depending on what the CEO and organization need. Elizabeth uses "chameleon" to describe this adaptive quality; the idea is also explicitly associated with Cameron Herold's description of the COO as a role that changes with the needs of the CEO and organization. See *Second in Command: Unleash the Power of Your COO* (2023).

## 8. Marketing's place in the operating system

Marketing remains a specialist function. But marketing also acts as an organization-wide source of external intelligence.

Marketing can tell the organization:

- what people want;
- what prospects are asking;
- how people find the organization;
- what messages resonate;
- which channels convert;
- what demand exists;
- what the market believes;
- what competitors and technology are changing.

That information affects strategy, sales, delivery, finance, capacity, product/service design, and client experience.

Marketing therefore belongs in the operating rhythm without becoming everyone's job in the sense of everyone doing marketing. Market orientation research (Kohli & Jaworski, 1990; Narver & Slater, 1990) establishes at the firm level that systematic gathering and response to market intelligence is associated with better performance. The specific argument here — that this intelligence should be embedded in the operating rhythm rather than held inside the marketing function — extends that research as a working theory. It is a practitioner hypothesis with a coherent evidence basis, not a directly validated research finding.

## 9. The 10× test

"Scalable" should not become shorthand for "10×."

A serious growth question asks:

- What actually changes at greater volume?
- What breaks?
- Where do bottlenecks move?
- Where does information flow fail?
- How do decision rights change?
- What happens to quality?
- What happens to client experience?
- What happens to cash flow?
- What happens to people?
- What does the organization itself need to become?

The goal is not to predict the future perfectly.

> **The goal is adaptive capacity: the ability to respond intelligently when the organization becomes different from what it is today.**

## 10. Influences and attribution

The "chameleon" description of the COO is an influence from Cameron Herold's *Second in Command: Unleash the Power of Your COO* (2023). Herold's work emphasizes that the COO role is not a single fixed template and can shift according to the needs of the CEO and organization. This framework is not intended to reproduce Herold's taxonomy; the reference is used to acknowledge an influence on the description of the COO role.

The Socratic framing is a practitioner interpretation: Elizabeth describes Paul Donovan's questioning and teaching style as helping people reason their way toward answers rather than simply giving them the answer. This is retained as an observation, not a claim about Paul's formal pedagogical method.

## 11. Evidence and research boundary

This framework is grounded first in Elizabeth Lemoine's practitioner experience and observations. A research pass was conducted on 2026-08-09 against the ten priority questions in `research-agenda-v0.1.md`. The following summarizes the research status of each major claim.

**Well-supported by existing research:**
- Leader posture influences team climate (emotional contagion, group affective tone) — bidirectional; research supports both directions.
- Psychological safety enables learning behaviour and error reporting (Edmondson, 1999, 2018) — requires accountability as a co-condition; neither alone is sufficient.
- Intellectual humility and learning orientation are associated with better leadership outcomes (Owens et al., 2013; Dweck).
- Adaptive capacity is distinct from scalability and matters more in changing environments (Teece et al., 1997; dynamic capabilities theory).
- Broken cross-functional information flow is a documented source of operational failure — the design goal is quality and accessibility of information, not volume.
- Organizational learning requires double-loop capacity (Argyris & Schön, 1978); defensive routines actively prevent it; deliberate conditions are required.
- Trust and structural verification coexist and reinforce each other; surveillance corrodes trust but systematic performance monitoring does not (Sitkin & Bies, 1994; Das & Teng, 1998).
- Market orientation at the firm level is strongly associated with performance (Kohli & Jaworski, 1990; Narver & Slater, 1990).

**Supported in direction, with label or framing adjustments made:**
- The inquiry-based operating style is well-researched under *humble inquiry* (Schein, 2013) and coaching methodology — not as the historical Socratic method.
- Curiosity as a trait is validated as *dispositional curiosity* (Kashdan, 2009) and maps to *learning orientation*. The practitioner term is retained alongside the research label.

**Practitioner working theories — logical extensions of established research, presented as hypotheses:**
- Market intelligence embedded in the operating rhythm (extends market orientation research).
- Customer concentration as a prompt for cross-functional diagnostic review (extends established financial concentration risk frameworks).

The research layer should validate, nuance, or challenge the practitioner model rather than merely confirm it. Where the framework extends beyond established research, it says so explicitly.
