# Strategy documents hide problems when prioritisation is avoided

## Observation

A strategy document that has not forced a prioritisation trade-off is not a strategy — it is a list of aspirations. Three reliable signals that the document is hiding the problem rather than resolving it: every priority is labeled "high"; the document requires a slide deck to be understood; nobody references it after the offsite.

The most useful thing a leadership team can do is agree explicitly on what they are not doing. "We'll get to that in Q3" is usually code for "we haven't made the call yet."

## Interpretation

This connects to the broader observation that strategic drift occurs in the gap between stated priorities and daily operating decisions. A document that avoids naming trade-offs will not close that gap — it will widen it, because the absence of a stated no means every competing priority remains viable.

The test for whether a strategy is working is not whether it is agreed upon in the room but whether it is referenced in decisions made outside the room, by people who were not in the room.

## Evidence Needed

- Examples where the absence of explicit prioritisation in strategy documents led to measurable resource conflicts.
- Evidence on how often "high priority" labels correlate with execution failure.
- Counterexamples: strategy documents that maintained broad priorities without clear trade-offs and still produced coherent execution.
