# Examples

These examples are preserved as evidence of the thinking. They should be converted into polished case studies only after factual details and confidentiality are reviewed.

## 1. Salesforce / dirty data

### Situation
Salesforce was expected to serve multiple functions, including development/fundraising, marketing, coaching, and event operations. Legacy data was dirty, manual entry was cumbersome, searching was inconsistent, and duplicate records accumulated.

### Consequences
- Existing people could appear to be new prospects.
- Development could approach existing supporters incorrectly.
- Marketing communications could be affected.
- Coaching could approach existing relationships as if they were new.
- Data-removal requests became harder to execute cleanly.
- The organization risked appearing not to know or recognize people it already knew.

### Operational response
- Move toward direct event-registration-to-Salesforce flows.
- Detect potential duplicates.
- Resolve duplicates closer to the point of entry.
- Restrict sensitive/manual data-upload processes to a small number of people.
- Create explicit procedures for uploads and matching.

### Why it matters
The issue looked like a database problem. Its consequences were relationship, marketing, fundraising, coaching, privacy, and trust problems.

## 2. Event invoicing / finance information flow

### Situation
Events involved contracts, delivery, and invoices. Information did not consistently travel from event creation and delivery to finance quickly enough.

### Consequences
Invoices were sometimes late. Clients pushed back when invoices arrived late. The organization could appear to be pursuing payment for something it had not properly invoiced.

### Operational response
- Map the flow of information.
- Bring finance into the process earlier.
- Establish bi-weekly finance coordination.
- Have the CFO review relevant contracts.
- Give accountants visibility through ClickUp subtasks.
- Track invoice creation, receipts, status, and blockers.
- Improve the timing of invoice preparation.

### Why it matters
"Accounts receivable" was not the whole problem. The underlying issue was cross-functional information flow.

## 3. Sales promise / delivery alignment

### Situation
Sales conversations can create perceived commitments even when a salesperson says only "I'll look into that."

### Operational response
- Train sales on what can and cannot be promised.
- Normalize saying "I don't know; I'll find out."
- Review contracts operationally before commitments become obligations.
- Occasionally observe sales calls or meetings.
- Document decisions made during conversations.
- Translate commitments into tasks, owners, and delivery checks.

### Why it matters
The client experiences a commitment from the organization, not an internal distinction between "promise" and "possibility."

## 4. Event incident response

### Situation
During an online event, an unauthorized participant entered and posted slurs, derogatory language, and threats. The team initially did not have enough people with the necessary controls to remove the participant and delete harmful posts quickly.

### Operational response
- Own the incident.
- Acknowledge potential harm.
- Apologize appropriately.
- Identify the control/access gap.
- Increase the number of people with the ability to respond.
- Create procedures for handling similar incidents.

### Learning
Some participants were frustrated by the delay; others noticed the response and appreciated that the organization took responsibility.

### Why it matters
The fix was not simply "be more careful." The system itself needed greater resilience and distributed response capability.

## 5. Graphic designer / communication bottleneck

### Situation
A graphic designer was being included in email chains of 50–60 messages and expected to determine what work was required. A task that should have taken approximately 15 minutes could require extensive reading and interpretation.

### Operational response
- Structured request process.
- Form-style documentation.
- Defined change process.
- Limited the number of people who could communicate directly with the designer.
- Short recurring check-ins.
- Shared content calendar.
- Visible marketing dashboards.

### Important lesson
A deliberate bottleneck can remove a larger bottleneck. Restricting direct access reduced noise and improved information quality.

## 6. Customer concentration

### Situation
A business has three or four customers representing a large proportion of revenue.

### Market-aware response
Do not treat this only as a financial ratio. Investigate:
- why those clients chose the business;
- what the business does well;
- whether clients would recommend it;
- whether their feedback can inform messaging;
- whether capacity is limiting acquisition;
- where prospects enter and exit the pipeline;
- whether marketing, positioning, sales, intake, or delivery is the constraint.

## 7. Northlight itself

### Situation
Northlight has not yet had a paying customer, so there is no client-outcome evidence to claim.

### What Elizabeth has done anyway
- Developed contracts.
- Developed an anticipated client playbook.
- Built marketing infrastructure.
- Rebuilt the blog.
- Learned SEO/AEO/GEO.
- Developed newsletter and LinkedIn activity.
- Built technical infrastructure.
- Applied privacy-first data collection.
- Practiced failure recovery and retrospective thinking.
- Designed systems intended to be ready when a client arrives.

### Important status
This is evidence of practitioner application, not evidence of commercial success.

### Observation
At Divine Renovation, Elizabeth experienced the organization as having enough operating health and prior practice in adaptation that it could respond during COVID.

### Potential interpretation

### Validation status
