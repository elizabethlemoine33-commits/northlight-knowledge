# Operations Built for Growth — Research Agenda

This file separates Elizabeth Lemoine's practitioner observations from claims that should be tested against external research before being presented as general organizational theory.

## Priority research questions

### 1. Leadership posture and organizational climate
Elizabeth observed that the operating leader's frustration, fear, risk aversion, confidence, empowerment, and forward-looking posture appeared to influence the wider team's posture. Research should examine emotional contagion, leader affect, organizational climate, and related leadership mechanisms.

### 2. Psychological safety, curiosity, and learning
The framework proposes that people need conditions in which they can ask questions, admit uncertainty, challenge assumptions, and learn from failure. Research should test relationships among psychological safety, learning behaviour, error reporting, innovation, and operational performance.

### 3. Curiosity, humility, and senior operating leadership
The framework treats curiosity plus humility as a core COO capability. Research should examine whether intellectual humility, inquiry-based leadership, sensemaking, and cross-functional problem solving are associated with better organizational outcomes.

**Research finding — 2026-08-09:** The organizational behavior literature supports intellectual humility and learning orientation (Dweck) as validated constructs associated with better leadership outcomes. "Curiosity" as a practitioner term maps onto *dispositional curiosity* (Kashdan, 2009) — a stable personality trait linked to sustained engagement, well-being, and meaning-making across the lifespan. These are compatible framings, not competing ones: intellectual humility and learning orientation name the research constructs; curiosity names the lived experience.

**Curiosity and cognitive health (practitioner note):** There is a documented connection between dispositional curiosity, sustained mental activity, and long-term cognitive health. The mechanism is *cognitive reserve* (Stern, 2002) — the brain's resilience to age-related change, built through education, intellectually stimulating work, and ongoing learning. People with higher cognitive reserve show symptoms of cognitive decline later, even when underlying neurological changes are similar. Intellectual engagement is identified in the research as a protective factor that may delay onset or slow progression of conditions such as dementia and Alzheimer's — not a prevention or cure, but a meaningful buffer.

The implication for the framework: curiosity is not only a leadership effectiveness trait. A COO who sustains intellectual curiosity across a career may also be protecting their own cognitive longevity. This is worth noting without overstating — the evidence is associational, not a guarantee. The "curiosity keeps you young" expression is colloquially imprecise but points at something real: curious people stay mentally active, and sustained mental activity has documented cognitive benefits.

### 4. Socratic questioning as an operating practice
Elizabeth describes a questioning style in which a leader helps a subject-matter expert reason toward an answer rather than simply supplying the answer. Research should distinguish the historical Socratic method from broader inquiry-based coaching and examine where the latter has evidence in leadership, coaching, and organizational learning.

### 5. Market orientation and operations
The framework argues that market intelligence should move through the whole operating system rather than remain inside marketing. Research should examine market orientation, customer orientation, cross-functional integration, and links to innovation, performance, and growth.

### 6. Adaptive capacity versus scalability
The framework deliberately distinguishes adaptive capacity from simply being able to handle higher volume. Research should examine organizational resilience, dynamic capabilities, organizational agility, and how firms redesign systems as their environment and strategy change.

### 7. Cross-functional information flow
The framework treats broken information flow as a recurring source of operational failure. Research should examine coordination mechanisms, information integration, boundary-spanning roles, and cross-functional team effectiveness.

### 8. Organizational learning and the learning spiral
The framework proposes that failures, retrospectives, client feedback, and operational experience should change the next iteration rather than merely close a ticket. Research should examine organizational learning, double-loop learning, after-action review, continuous improvement, and knowledge retention.

### 9. Trust and verification
The framework uses "trust and verify" as an alternative to both micromanagement and blind trust. Research should examine trust, control systems, psychological safety, accountability, and appropriate verification mechanisms.

### 10. Customer concentration as an operating diagnostic
The framework treats a small number of customers accounting for a large share of revenue as more than a financial metric: it can be a prompt to investigate marketing, sales, positioning, capacity, delivery, and client experience. Research should distinguish established financial concentration risk from this broader diagnostic interpretation.

## Research standard

External research should be used to validate, nuance, or challenge the framework. It should not be used simply to decorate a practitioner model with citations. Where evidence is mixed or absent, the framework should say so.
