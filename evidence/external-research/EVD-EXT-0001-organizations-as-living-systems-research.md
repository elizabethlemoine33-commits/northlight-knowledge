# Organizations as Living Systems: Research Synthesis

## Purpose

This is external evidence for the core principle "Organisations Are Living Systems." It maps the intellectual tradition behind that principle — where it comes from, who's active in it now, what's contested, and what a practitioner can safely build on — commissioned to check Northlight's independently-developed worldview against the field it happens to sit inside.

## The lineage

Three branches feed the tradition, and they don't fully agree with each other.

**Systems theory**: Ludwig von Bertalanffy (*General System Theory*, 1968) — organizations as open systems that hold their shape through continuous exchange with an environment. Daniel Katz and Robert Kahn (*The Social Psychology of Organizations*, 1966) — first serious application to organizational behaviour. Tom Burns and G.M. Stalker (*The Management of Innovation*, 1961) — mechanistic vs. organic structures. James Grier Miller (*Living Systems*, 1978) — a 20-subsystem taxonomy across eight hierarchical levels, the most literal reading of the metaphor in the tradition.

**Autopoiesis and cybernetics**: Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela (*Autopoiesis and Cognition*, 1980) — living systems as self-producing networks. Niklas Luhmann (*Social Systems*, 1995) — applied autopoiesis to society, arguing organizations are made of recursive decisions, not people. Stafford Beer (*Brain of the Firm*, 1972) — the Viable System Model.

**Practitioners and popularizers**: Gareth Morgan (*Images of Organization*, 1986) — organism is one metaphor among eight; no single metaphor should be held too tightly. Peter Senge (*The Fifth Discipline*, 1990) — systems thinking applied to organizational learning. Margaret Wheatley (*Leadership and the New Science*, 1992). Fritjof Capra (*The Web of Life*, 1996; *The Hidden Connections*, 2002). Arie de Geus (*The Living Company*, 1997) — the most evidence-anchored entry, built on Shell's internal longitudinal research into centenarian companies. Frederic Laloux (*Reinventing Organizations*, 2014) — translates the tradition into an operating model built from field research (Buurtzorg, Morning Star), not theory-first.

**Complexity theory** — a close cousin, not identical. Ralph Stacey developed "complex responsive processes of relating": organizations as patterns of human interaction, not entities with a self. Dave Snowden built Cynefin, a sense-making framework matching decision approach to problem type. Mary Uhl-Bien (Complexity Leadership Theory) and Glenda Eoyang (Human Systems Dynamics) apply complexity science to leadership directly.

## Where the field is now vs. 10-20 years ago

Ten to twenty years ago this was mostly critique — explaining why command-and-control wasn't working, aimed at readers already skeptical of the machine model. Laloux's 2014 book was the inflection point: from "better metaphor" to "specific operating model, with named companies running it." That produced a live practitioner community (Corporate Rebels, annual summits, Buurtzorg as reference case) — and a backlash from inside that community: Corporate Rebels' own "Teal Ain't Real" piece warns against turning Laloux's descriptive stages into dogma.

Ralph Stacey died in 2021; his line continues through Hertfordshire's Complexity and Management Centre. Dave Snowden and the Cynefin Co remain the most active, visible complexity practice today. Snowden has said he built later Cynefin refinements partly in response to Stacey's own regret about how the Stacey Matrix got flattened into a tool that removed the paradox it was meant to preserve — a rare, honest instance of one thinker publicly correcting course based on a peer's critique.

## Complexity theory vs. the living-systems frame — the real divergence

Both reject planning-and-controlling an organization like a machine; both take emergence seriously. The divergence is ontological. The living-systems/autopoiesis branch treats the organization as a bounded *thing* with something like a self — an identity, a boundary, a memory, something that can be sensitive or numb, healthy or sick. Stacey's complex responsive process theory rejects that move directly: there is no organization behind the conversations, only the ongoing pattern of people relating, and reifying "the organization" as an entity — even a living one — is the same category error the machine metaphor made, in softer language. Snowden's Cynefin mostly sidesteps the ontological fight; it doesn't claim to describe what an organization *is*, only how to match decision-making approach to how predictable a given problem is right now.

## What's contested

**The physicality argument** — Maturana and Varela themselves said Luhmann's social application of autopoiesis doesn't hold, because autopoiesis requires a physical boundary (a cell membrane) organizations don't have. Defenders answer that communicative/semantic boundaries do the same job. Unresolved, including among the founders.

**The erasure of agency** — critics (notably John Mingers) argue Luhmann's communication-based model reduces individuals to the system's "environment." Defenders say this anti-anthropocentric move deliberately explains organizational logic that exceeds any individual's intent. Awkward fit for advisory work that's supposed to be about people.

**The tautology charge** — if a living system is defined as "that which reproduces itself," the theory can't be falsified: anything that survives proves it, anything that dies wasn't really living enough. The frame works better as a diagnostic lens than a predictive science.

**The harmony trap** — Gareth Morgan's own warning, from inside the tradition: biological metaphors imply a natural, functional unity real organizations don't have. A body's organs don't compete for power the way departments do. Leaning too hard on "organism" language risks masking politics, exploitation, and conflict as things to be metabolized away rather than named and addressed.

A smaller point: the tradition is generally thin on empirical rigour. Most support is case study and metaphor, not controlled comparison. De Geus's book is the exception, grounded in Shell's actual internal research.

## What transfers to small-to-mid-size organizations

De Geus's four traits — sensitivity, cohesion, tolerance, conservative financing — work almost as-is as a diagnostic, because they're plain-language and don't require buying the full ontological argument first. Laloux's field cases are unusually built for small scale (Buurtzorg teams capped around ten to twenty people) — a genuine advantage over management theory that's disproportionately built from large-company research and scaled down awkwardly. Caution: self-management structures have a documented failure mode at small scale too — Zappos's Holacracy rollout is the most-cited cautionary tale. Cynefin's simple/complicated/complex/chaotic sort is the single most immediately actionable piece for SME work, separate from the biological metaphor entirely: complex problems (most of what a growing SME actually faces) call for small safe-to-fail experiments, not a five-year plan.

## Five claims a practitioner can build on

1. Organizations behave like open systems, not closed machines — the least contested claim in the tradition.
2. Learning speed is a survival variable, not overhead — de Geus's Shell research is the best-grounded evidence available.
3. In genuinely complex situations, sensing and small experiments beat planning and best practice — Cynefin's core, most operationally actionable claim.
4. Small units with local autonomy adapt faster than centrally controlled ones — up to a point; fails without a strong shared identity to hold it together (de Geus's cohesion, Laloux's wholeness).
5. The metaphor's value is diagnostic, not predictive — the claim that inoculates against the tautology critique before anyone raises it.
