# Operations Built for Growth

## Operations is not simply about keeping the organization running.

Operations is the backbone of an organization: the infrastructure and nervous system through which people, information, decisions, commitments, and learning move.

That means operations cannot be understood in isolation.

If you do not understand the organization's strategy, you cannot build toward it. If you do not understand how growth happens, you can build processes that work beautifully for a small organization and quietly constrain its future. If you do not understand finance, you may miss how an operational problem becomes a cash-flow problem. If you do not understand marketing, you may miss the signals that tell you what customers want, where demand is coming from, or why an organization has only three or four customers.

The work of operations is not merely to make today's organization more efficient. It is to help build an organization capable of becoming what it is meant to become.

That is what I mean by **Operations Built for Growth**.

## The organization is a system, not a collection of departments

Organizations often appear on an org chart as separate departments: marketing, sales, finance, operations, delivery, technology, people. The work itself is rarely that clean.

Marketing creates expectations that sales has to understand. Sales makes commitments that delivery has to fulfill. Delivery creates information finance needs in order to invoice. Finance affects the organization's capacity to hire and invest. Client experience affects retention, referrals, reputation, and revenue. Data affects marketing, fundraising, sales, service, and privacy.

The results are interconnected even when the departments are not.

Operations may be a department, but operational thinking cannot be confined to the operations department. Operations has hands in everything because information has to move between everything.

I think of operations as the spine and nervous system. Strategy provides direction; operations helps that direction travel through the organization. Marketing is another useful part of the metaphor: it is like the blood or flow carrying external intelligence and demand into the system. The metaphor is not a literal organizational taxonomy. It is a way of remembering that an organization is an interconnected living system made of specialized parts that have to work together.

## Build for what the organization is becoming

One of the easiest operational mistakes is to build for what exists today.

A ten-person organization can get away with informal communication because everyone roughly knows what everyone else is doing. People see the Slack messages. They hear conversations in the office. A subject-matter expert may also be a generalist who knows how to solve a dozen other problems because they have learned along the way.

That changes as the organization grows.

At seventy-five people, nobody can read every message. People no longer automatically know what another department is doing. Information needs intentional pathways. Cross-functional communication has to be designed rather than assumed.

But building for growth does not mean blindly building for "10×."

I have little patience for the idea that a system is growth-ready simply because someone says it can handle ten times the volume. What actually changes at ten times? What breaks? Where do bottlenecks move? What happens to information flow, decision rights, quality, cash flow, staffing, client experience, and risk? Does the organization itself need to become different?

The goal is not to predict the future perfectly.

The goal is to build **adaptive capacity**.

Some systems need strong controls. Privacy, security, financial integrity, charitable status, tax requirements, contracts, and material legal or organizational risk are not places for casual experimentation. Other systems should evolve as technology, customer expectations, markets, and the organization change.

So I do not want to build merely "stronger systems." Strong can sound rigid. I want **adaptive systems**: systems with enough structure to protect what matters and enough process and capability to respond intelligently when reality changes.

## The COO does not have to be the expert

The COO does not need to be the best marketer, accountant, technologist, salesperson, or delivery expert in the organization.

The COO needs to understand enough to ask good questions and understand the answers.

Sometimes that means saying, "Explain it to me like I'm five." That is not a failure of expertise. It is part of the job.

The COO is a generalist integrator. Depending on the organization and the CEO, that can mean being an executor, moderator, enforcer, devil's advocate, mentor, counterpart, or second-in-command. The role changes because the organization changes. Cameron Herold's *Second in Command: Unleash the Power of Your COO* (2023) is an important influence on this view of the COO as a role that changes with the needs of the CEO and organization. I use "chameleon" to describe that adaptive quality; I am not trying to reproduce Herold's taxonomy here.

The COO also needs humility. Curiosity is not much use if you are too proud to admit that you do not understand something. A COO should be able to say, "Explain it to me like I'm five," and genuinely listen to the answer. That is not a failure of expertise. It is part of the job.

I also think there is something distinctly Socratic about good operating leadership. The best operators I have worked with often ask questions from different angles until the person closest to the work can see what is missing or arrive at the answer themselves. Paul Donovan used to teach and coach this way. The point was not simply to give people the answer; it was to help them learn how to reason through the problem.

The COO's value is often in the seams between functions: noticing that sales promised something delivery cannot provide, that finance is waiting for information nobody knows it needs, that marketing is generating demand the organization cannot fulfill, or that a database problem is quietly damaging client relationships.

The best operators I have worked with were often people who asked very good questions. They did not necessarily tell the subject-matter expert what the answer was. They asked questions from different angles until the person doing the work could see what was missing.

That requires curiosity and humility.

If you do not want to learn, ask questions, or admit you do not know something, operations is probably the wrong work.

## Marketing belongs in the operating rhythm

Marketing is a specialist discipline. Marketing people should do marketing.

But marketing cannot be left entirely inside the marketing department because marketing is also a source of organizational intelligence.

Marketing tells us what customers want, what prospects are asking, how people find us, which messages resonate, what channels convert, where demand exists, and what the market is changing. A market-aware operator needs to understand enough marketing to read those signals and ask useful questions.

That does not mean becoming an SEO specialist or a paid-ad expert. It means being able to look at a dashboard and ask why different systems report different traffic, whether the traffic is human or bot activity, what conversion actually means, which channel is producing meaningful results, and why a particular campaign deserves more or less investment.

It also means understanding that marketing creates expectations for the rest of the organization.

Marketing cannot promise next-day delivery if sales and operations cannot deliver it. Marketing cannot offer a price finance cannot support. Marketing cannot create demand without considering whether the organization has the capacity to serve it.

Marketing belongs in the operating rhythm because the organization is one system.

## Customer concentration is an operational problem, too

Imagine an organization has three or four customers and those customers represent most of its revenue. That is obviously a financial concentration risk. But it is also a diagnostic question.

Why only three or four?

What do those customers love? Would they recommend us? What did we do right? What outcomes did we create? What could we learn from them? What evidence could we use in our messaging or case studies?

Then look at the entire journey. How do people discover the organization? What happens when they show interest? How do they connect? How are their needs understood? How are they matched to the offer? Where do prospects disappear? Is the constraint marketing, positioning, sales, intake, delivery capacity, staffing, or something else?

A small customer base can therefore reveal something much larger about the operating system.

## Every promise needs a path to delivery

A promise does not have to be a formal contractual commitment to affect a client. If someone on a sales call says, "I'll look into that," the client may hear a promise.

Operations should help make sure promises have an operational path to delivery.

That can include training sales on what can and cannot be promised, reviewing contracts, associating commitments with owners and tasks, building dashboards, and checking whether delivery actually happened.

A simple operational chain is:

**promise → system → owner → delivery → verification**

The point is not to create bureaucracy that holds up projects. It is to prevent the organization from discovering after the fact that nobody was responsible for the thing it promised.

## Good operations knows when to say no

Operations is sometimes perceived as the bureaucracy that gets in the way. Sometimes that perception is justified. Sometimes the block exists for a very good reason.

There are constraints that are not negotiable: tax and regulatory requirements, charitable status, privacy obligations, financial controls, contracts, and material risk.

Then there are design choices: how a database is structured, how a form is designed, how a workflow works, what access someone receives, what information a dashboard contains, or how a service is delivered.

The operator needs to know the difference.

The question should not simply be, "Can we do exactly what you asked?"

It should be:

> **What are you actually trying to achieve, and how can we get there within the parameters in which we have to operate?**

Sometimes the answer is no. Sometimes the answer is a redesigned process. Sometimes it is a new agreement, a new form, a new report, a new system, or a new way of working.

Saying no for a legitimate reason can actually build trust — especially when operations works hard to find another path forward.

## When something goes wrong, change the system

I believe you should own it.

A good response to a failure is to acknowledge what happened, apologize when appropriate, identify what went wrong, determine why it happened, put a plan in place to prevent recurrence, communicate that plan to the people affected, and then actually live out what you said you would do.

A single failure is one thing. A repeated failure becomes a pattern.

The operational response should therefore be more than fixing the immediate problem. It should ask what allowed the problem to happen and what needs to change so that the next person does not encounter the same problem.

That is why retrospectives matter. What did we say we would do? What did we deliver? Were there gaps? What worked? What did not? What did we learn? What did the client experience? What changes now?

The same principle applies to major incidents. When an online event was disrupted by abusive behaviour, the problem was not simply to remove the person. We also had to understand why the response took too long, improve access to the necessary controls, communicate what had changed, and own the experience with the people affected.

Handled well, accountability can strengthen trust rather than destroy it.

## The operating cycle is a spiral

This is not a flat loop.

The model I am developing is:

**Awareness → Sense → Build → Connect → Learn → ↑**

**Awareness** means looking outward: beyond your function, across the organization, toward clients and the market, and toward the wider world.

**Sense** means understanding what you are seeing. Ask questions. Diagnose. Find the real constraint. Distinguish symptom from cause.

**Build** means turning that understanding into capability: a process, system, policy, technology, training, dashboard, role, or operating rhythm.

**Connect** means making sure the change actually works within the whole organization. Information has to reach the right people. Ownership has to be clear. A fix in one place cannot create a new problem somewhere else.

**Learn** means turning experience into organizational memory. Own the failure. Understand it. Change the system. Verify the change. Carry the learning forward.

Then the spiral begins again — from a different place.

Everything you have done becomes part of what you know next.

## What good operations should feel like

If operations is working well, I would want people to say:

> I have clarity on some things. And when I don't, I know where to go and I can safely ask the questions.

I would want people to feel that ideation and innovation are good things. That it is safe to bring the right people together, even when they are outside your department. That you do not have to be in every conversation to know what matters because the organization has enough visibility built into its systems.

And when something goes wrong, people should know that accountability is real but learning is also real.

That is where **trust and verify** matters. The goal is neither micromanagement nor blind trust. It is confidence that the system and the people within it are doing what they should, with enough visibility to verify what matters.

I have also seen how much the human condition of the operating environment matters. One of my former leaders told me that when I was frustrated, nervous, or risk-averse, the team tended to become those things too; when I was confident, empowered, and forward-thinking, those qualities spread as well. I treat that as an important practitioner observation rather than a universal claim. It is something worth researching.

But the practical lesson is clear to me: operations is not only process. It is also the conditions in which people make decisions, ask questions, take appropriate risks, learn, and work together.

## Operations Built for Growth

The point is not to build an organization that never has problems.

The point is to build one that can **sense problems, understand them, respond intelligently, learn, and become more capable as a result.**

That is why I do not define growth simply as more customers or more revenue.

Growth also means greater capacity, greater capability, greater reach, greater resilience, greater profitability, and greater ability to adapt and respond to opportunity.

You cannot always guarantee that the organization will be dealt a great hand. You can, however, maintain enough capability and adaptive capacity to be ready when the next hand is better.

**Build for what the organization is becoming.**

Not merely what it is today.

And not an imagined version of ten times today.

Build an organization capable of becoming different without losing its ability to function, learn, and deliver — with enough structure to protect what matters and enough adaptive capacity to respond intelligently when reality changes.
