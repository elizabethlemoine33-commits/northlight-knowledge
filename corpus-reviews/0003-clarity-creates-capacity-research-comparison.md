## Key Themes

**The clarity framework is a complicated-domain tool, and didn't say so.** Dave Snowden's Cynefin framework distinguishes complicated problems (knowable through analysis, best practice applies) from complex ones (knowable only in retrospect, requiring small safe-to-fail experiments before anything can be clarified). Northlight's five-step clarity sequence — outcomes, ownership, decisions, information flow, measures — is built for the complicated case. It was previously silent on which domain it was designed for, which means it could be, and likely has been, applied to genuinely complex problems where premature clarity manufactures false confidence rather than real capacity.

**Requisite variety sharpens the risk.** Ashby's law — a system's capacity to respond to its environment is bounded by its own internal variety — gives a mechanism for why this goes wrong, not just a warning that it might. Removing "unnecessary" complexity is only safe if what's removed really is unnecessary; if it's actually the organisation's adaptive repertoire for an unpredictable environment, clarity work reduces capacity while feeling like it's increasing it.

**Weick's sensemaking reframes the sequence, not just the caveat.** "How can I know what I think until I see what I say?" suggests that for ambiguous situations, clarity is often a retrospective account built from action, not a precondition for it. This argues for something more specific than "be careful": in the complex case, the clarity framework's own steps (outcomes, ownership, measures) should be treated as things that get discovered through probing, not things decided in advance and then executed against.

**What the repo already had right.** The clarity framework and worldview document already distinguished "unnecessary complexity" from complexity generally — the instinct not to oversimplify was present before this review. What was missing was an operational way to tell the two apart before acting, which this review adds as an explicit Step 0.

## Candidate Understandings

- The clarity framework needs a domain check before it runs, not just a caveat after. Added as Step 0 in `organisational-clarity-framework.md`.
- "Unnecessary complexity" (safe to remove) and "requisite variety" (the organisation's real adaptive capacity) can look identical from the outside until tested — this is a live risk in client engagements, not just a theoretical one, and worth watching for directly.

## Candidate Principles

- No new core principle proposed. Principle #3 is annotated with the domain-check caveat rather than replaced.

## Open Questions

- Has the clarity framework ever been run against a problem that turned out to be complex rather than complicated? If a past engagement fits, it should become the first real evidence for `OBS-2026-2005` and move its confidence from medium toward high.
- Does "Step 0: Check the domain first" slow the framework down enough in practice that it needs a fast, non-academic version — a gut-check question rather than a formal Cynefin sort — to stay usable in a live client conversation?
- Should the same domain-check caveat be added to `decision-quality-framework.md` and `advisory-engagement-loop.md`, since both likely carry the same complicated-domain assumption?

## Follow-up Work

1. Watch the next few client engagements for a case where the clarity sequence was applied to something that was actually complex, and log it as evidence either way.
2. Draft a one-line, plain-language version of the Cynefin domain check for client-facing use — something closer to "do we already know the answer, or do we need to try something small first?" — since the internal research language won't land in a client conversation.
3. Consider whether this same research-comparison pass is worth running against `decision-quality-framework.md` next, since decision quality and clarity sit close together in client work.
