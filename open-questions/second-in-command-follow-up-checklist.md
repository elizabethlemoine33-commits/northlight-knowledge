## Before publishing the review

### Verify every page number
Elizabeth's notes were made while reading her physical copy. Check:
- chapter;
- page;
- exact wording;
- edition.

Do not assume a web edition uses identical pagination.

### Verify direct quotations
If a sentence appears in quotation marks in the eventual review:
- compare against the physical book;
- copy exact wording;
- use the correct page;
- keep quoted passages short.

### Distinguish author claims from Elizabeth's interpretation

Label these carefully.

**Herold's concepts**
- active inventory;
- seven COO types;
- 90-day onboarding;
- role/title alignment;
- CEO/COO trust;
- delegation;
- working with the COO;
- organizational-stage fit.

**Elizabeth's synthesis**
- "CEO moves things forward; COO makes it possible."
- the COO as the CEO's person;
- asymmetry of the relationship;
- December absence as a systems stress test;
- "strong sense of self, low need for ego gratification";
- working yourself out of a job as success;
- values alignment as an execution condition.

### Avoid overclaiming

Do not say:
- "The book proves..."
- "All COOs..."
- "Every CEO..."

Prefer:
- "Herold argues..."
- "In my experience..."
- "What resonated with me..."
- "I would extend this by saying..."
- "The book made me reconsider..."

# Possible additional research

## 1. COO role evolution by company size
Research whether there is empirical support for the idea that COO profiles change at specific organizational stages.

This could strengthen Elizabeth's argument about stage-dependent fit.

## 2. CEO/COO relational dynamics
Look for research on:
- executive dyads;
- CEO/top-team trust;
- psychological safety;
- role ambiguity;
- power sharing;
- executive succession.

## 3. Fractional COO economics
Research:
- typical fractional COO engagement structures;
- retainer models;
- engagement duration;
- scope;
- transition from fractional to permanent;
- compensation/runway considerations.

## 4. Founder succession and operator transitions
Explore whether founder-led companies commonly need different operating leadership at different stages.

## 5. "Second-in-command" vs Chief of Staff
This could become a useful Northlight distinction.

Questions:
- Where does authority sit?
- Is the role C-suite?
- Does the person own operations?
- Can they direct other executives?
- Are they primarily CEO leverage or organizational integrator?
- When does Chief of Staff become a COO?
