# EOS Rocks and OKR Retrospective

## Summary

Retrospective interview with Elizabeth Lemoine (August 2026) on the use of EOS-style Rocks at a former organization during 2019–2021, supplemented by internal documents from that period. The interview also covered a later OKR implementation and what practices survived both experiences.

**Publication status:** Internal only. Do not publish names, organizational details, metrics, or project identifiers without a separate anonymization and editing pass. The strongest reusable material is the operating insight, not the historical detail.

---

## Context

The Ops team began using EOS-style Rocks after the organization had developed a set of five values but before a strategic plan was available. The senior leadership group had developed the values; the wider team then translated them into practical behaviours and actions. The Ops team selected three values and identified concrete actions it could take to live those values out.

The team was also operating through major systems changes, including a recent Salesforce launch and continued use of G Suite, followed shortly by a transition to Microsoft 365.

The Rocks mechanism provided a way to create finite priorities while the larger strategic picture was still developing.

---

## Why Rocks Were Adopted

A team member with strong experience and enthusiasm for EOS Traction introduced the concepts, including Rocks, through books, explanation, and discussion. The useful idea was that a Rock was a significant thing that needed to be accomplished within a defined period, supported by meaningful milestones and measurable indicators.

The team did not treat the first implementation as perfect. They explicitly expected to refine goals and metrics as they learned. They aimed to keep team Rocks to roughly 3–7 per quarter.

---

## How the Team Worked

The team used whiteboarding and discussion before turning priorities into formal slides. Each Rock had some combination of:

- a defined goal;
- meaningful milestones or supporting tasks;
- metrics or evidence of progress;
- an identified owner;
- regular review.

The Rocks were printed and kept physically visible in the department lead's office. Team members could add checkmarks, notes, or updates. Progress was discussed during regular team meetings and reviewed at the end of the quarter.

---

## What Changed

The team developed a clearer distinction between:

- work important enough to be a Rock;
- work that was important but not a Rock;
- ideas that might be useful later.

A parking-lot concept allowed ideas to be retained without allowing them to become current commitments.

The mechanism also clarified ownership across functional boundaries. Someone could be responsible for advancing a project while the department lead remained accountable for the overall outcome and other stakeholders were consulted or informed as appropriate. This effectively created some RACI-like behaviour without formally introducing a RACI exercise.

---

## Accountability and Trust

The team experienced accountability as ownership rather than surveillance. A person could own a Rock and provide concise updates while the manager remained available for advice, feedback, resources, and escalation.

One example involved ownership of work around a newly implemented CRM. The department lead retained ultimate accountability while another team member became the operational owner of the system-related work. This increased capability, confidence, and clarity while reducing the need for the department lead to personally manage every detail.

---

## Failure, Learning, and Adaptation

The team did not treat an unsuccessful Rock as something to disguise. When a priority did not progress, the review documented that reality. Some items were explicitly recognized as premature. The point was to learn what the team had misjudged and decide what should happen next.

The retrospective preference is to avoid treating "failure" as the primary label. A failed attempt can be successful if it produces useful learning and informs the next iteration.

---

## COVID as a Stress Test

When the organization moved home during the pandemic, the larger strategic environment became highly uncertain. Major plans changed or disappeared, but the Rocks gave the Ops team a continuing backbone of concrete work.

The Rocks did not solve the organization's strategic uncertainty. They provided enough operational structure for the team to keep working, adapting, and supporting the organization while broader direction was unsettled.

---

## Limits of the Mechanism

Rocks could not create organizational vision or strategy. The CEO and senior leadership had responsibility for establishing where the organization was going. Operations could help determine how to move from the current state toward that destination, but should not quietly invent the destination.

When the strategic plan was delayed or unclear, the Ops team could maintain structure and execution but could not manufacture strategic clarity.

---

## Later Experience With OKRs

A later organizational shift introduced OKRs. The retrospective assessment is not that OKRs are inherently inferior to Rocks. The problem was implementation and organizational context.

The implementation suffered from:

- unclear understanding of objectives versus key results;
- weak links between departmental objectives and organizational strategy;
- too many participants in large virtual review meetings;
- limited ability to challenge or deprioritize proposed objectives;
- insufficient team-level teaching and deliberation;
- weak ownership and accountability;
- lack of a parking-lot mechanism;
- insufficient organizational trust and clarity.

The retrospective view is that a sound methodology can perform badly when adopted before the organization has sufficient strategic and relational clarity to use it well.

---

## What Survived

The following practices remain part of current working habits:

- maintain a parking lot for ideas that are not current priorities;
- deliberately limit active priorities;
- define why a piece of work matters before doing it;
- make ownership visible;
- look for measurable outcomes or evidence of "good enough";
- create review points for experiments and initiatives;
- adapt plans when evidence changes;
- use tools to support thinking rather than substitute for it.

The current practice uses ClickUp, project dashboards, project lists, build logs, and structured review sessions, but the underlying discipline is independent of any specific software or methodology.

---

## Case Study Notes: EOS Rocks During Organizational Change

### Thesis

A small operations team used EOS-style Rocks to translate organizational values into concrete quarterly action, create visible ownership, and maintain operational continuity during a period of strategic uncertainty and rapid organizational change.

The experience showed both the usefulness and the limits of quarterly priority mechanisms: Rocks could create operational clarity and accountability, but they could not supply missing organizational vision or strategy.

### Starting Conditions

The organization had recently developed five values through senior leadership work. The broader team was then asked to consider how those values could be lived out through concrete actions. The Ops team selected three values and translated them into specific actions. Examples included cross-training, Salesforce and G Suite capability building, and improvements to customer-service practices.

### Review and Honesty

Quarterly review was not treated as a ceremony for declaring success. The team recorded misses and premature assumptions. One customer-service priority was explicitly described as a miss and retained for reevaluation rather than being presented as completed.

This is a key case-study lesson: a priority system only creates useful accountability if people can tell the truth about progress.

### What the Experience Demonstrates

The case does not demonstrate that EOS is universally superior to other methods.

It demonstrates that a priority mechanism can work well when it has:

- limited priorities;
- clear ownership;
- measurable evidence;
- visible progress;
- regular review;
- permission to revise;
- a mechanism for deferring ideas;
- honest reporting;
- sufficient trust.

### Later Comparison With OKRs

A later OKR implementation attempted to connect departmental objectives to organizational strategy. The retrospective assessment was that the methodology was poorly implemented in an environment with insufficient strategic clarity and organizational trust.

The contrast suggests that methodology selection is less important than the conditions in which the methodology is introduced and the quality of the implementation.
