# When project tone shifts, the problem is almost always structure not people

## Observation

When the emotional register of a project changes — conversations become careful, emails get longer, responses slow, people hedge — the instinctive read is that a people problem has developed. In most cases this is wrong. The signal points to a structural failure: roles not defined clearly, accountability that landed somewhere nobody explicitly agreed to, decision rights that were assumed rather than assigned.

## Interpretation

This matters diagnostically. If the response to a tone shift is a people intervention (a conversation, a performance concern, a team-dynamic exercise), the actual problem goes unaddressed. If the response is a structural audit (who owns what, where did accountability land, what was actually agreed), the underlying issue becomes visible and solvable.

People behave carefully when they are unclear about what they are responsible for and what happens if they get it wrong. That is a systems failure, not a character failure.

## Evidence Needed

- Examples where tone-shift diagnosis correctly identified a structural problem.
- Examples where a people intervention was applied to a structural problem and failed to resolve it.
- Counterexamples: genuine people-level dynamics (trust breakdown, interpersonal conflict) presenting similarly but requiring different intervention.
